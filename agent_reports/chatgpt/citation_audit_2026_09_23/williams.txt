Intention-like representations in language models?

Iwan Williams1

Received: 13 October 2025 / Accepted: 21 August 2026
© The Author(s) 2026



Abstract
A growing chorus of AI researchers and philosophers posit internal representations
in large language models (LLMs). But how do these representations relate to the
kinds of mental states we routinely ascribe to our fellow humans? While some
research has focused on belief- or knowledge-like states in LLMs, there has been
comparatively little focus on the question of whether LLMs have intentions. I sur-
vey five properties that have been associated with intentions in the philosophical
literature, and assess two candidate classes of LLM representations against this set
of features. The result is mixed: current evidence suggests that LLMs have repre-
sentations that are intention-like in many—perhaps surprising—respects, but they
differ from human intentions in important ways. Deciding whether to classify these
representations as intentions will require careful consideration of the costs and pay-
offs of extending the concept beyond the paradigm human case.

Keywords Intentions · Large language models · Mental representation · Agency ·
Artificial intelligence

   This neglect of intentions is striking for several reasons. Firstly, intentions play
an important role in many theories of communication. Linguistic capacities such as
assertion, and even the ability to make meaningful utterances, are widely held to
require intentions of one kind or another (e.g. intentions to perform a speech act, to

being thwarted, the discovery of LLM intentions would potentially be relevant to
their status as moral patients (Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini, 2025). And finally, inten-

systems are not responsible for their behaviour, sometimes on the grounds that they
lack intentions (Johnson, 2006; van Woudenberg et al., 2024).3

2 Five characteristic features of human intentions

In what follows, I will assume a causal-representational view of intentions, on which
intentions are (i) implemented by representations that (ii) play a causal role in the
production of actions. This is not a universally accepted assumption. Philosophers
have variously held that intention ascriptions provide teleological rather than causal
explanations (Wilson, 1980), that intentions amount to a form of practical self-
knowledge (Anscombe, 1957), or being in progress towards an action (Thompson,
2008). Nevertheless, the causal-representational view has substantial attractions and
has been dubbed “the standard story” of action (Velleman, 1992, p. 461) for good
reason. It clearly accommodates a distinction between reasons that could rationalise
an action and the reason for which an agent acted. It is also the account most naturally
amenable to empirical investigation using the methods of cognitive science (Mylo-
poulos & Pacherie, 2019) and the AI interpretability methods to be introduced below.
Thus, while far from the only approach, assuming a causal-representational account
provides a productive starting point for an inquiry into LLM intentions.
   I will discuss five properties widely associated with intentions: (1) directive func-
tion, (2) degrees of distality, (3) degrees of abstraction, (4) commitment and (5)
planning. Some of these describe representation-level properties, characteristic of
intentions themselves; others are system-level features, to which intentions make a
characteristic contribution. I make no claim as to whether these features are indi-

2.1 Directive function

Representations can play different roles in an agent’s cognitive economy. One basic
distinction is between descriptive and directive representations, which differ in their
“direction of fit” (Anscombe, 1957; Humberstone, 1992). Descriptive representa-
tions, such as beliefs, function to represent the way the world antecedently is, and
thus have a “mind-to-world” direction of fit.6 Directive representations, like desires
and intentions, have the function of bringing the world into conformity with their
content—a “world-to-mind”, direction of fit (Millikan, 1995; Shea, 2018).
   An important question concerns the strength of the relevant notion of “function”.
A role-function (Craver, 2013; Cummins, 1975) is simply a causal role played by
some component in a system with respect to an explanandum of interest. Role-func-
tions can accommodate the possibility of functions that are not always performed, but
they do not underwrite any meaningful kind of normativity (i.e. a distinction between
malfunction and correct functioning). By contrast, a proper function is a causal role
that has been selected for, e.g. stabilised through natural selection or learning.
   From this, we can derive two alternative accounts of directive representation. On a
less demanding account, directive representations have the role-function of bringing
the world into conformity with their content.7 On a more demanding account, direc-
tive representations have the proper function of doing so—i.e. this role was selected
for. This distinction will be important when assessing LLM representations in Sect.

2.2 Degrees of distality

Theorists sometimes distinguish between distal (or prospective) and proximal (or
immediate) intentions (Mele, 1992; Pacherie & Haggard, 2011). Proximal intentions
are intentions to act now. They initiate actions, and thus occur a very short time
before the action itself. A football player seeing an open goal forms an intention to
shoot, causing the action moments later. Distal intentions, on the other hand, are
intentions to act at some time in the future—either specified, or as yet unspecified. I
can intend now to take a sip of coffee when I finish this paragraph. I may make plans
this morning to go shopping later this evening. And while in my 20s I might intend
that I’ll retire when I’m older, without yet knowing exactly when. It is characteristic
of human intentions that they span a spectrum from distal to proximal, with inten-
tions at different timescales coordinating in the planning and execution of action
(Mylopoulos & Pacherie, 2019).

2.3 Degrees of abstraction

Intentions are often abstract and partial (Pacherie, 2008; Bratman, 1987, p. 31). They
are not always instructions for a very precise behaviour or bodily movement. There
are myriad ways of realising an intention to get a well-paying job. Not only are there
many such jobs, there are many ways of getting those jobs, and the set of bodily
movement sequences that could result in the intention being satisfied is functionally
infinite. Even a relatively concrete intention, say, to pick up my mug could be car-
ried out in many different ways: How should I get from my current position to being
within reaching distance? Should I grab it firmly or loosely? How high should I raise
it up?
    The point here is not that intentions are always abstract (or always distal)—many
human intentions are specific (and proximal). The point is that it is characteristic of
paradigmatic intention-possessing systems—adult humans—that our intentions run
the gamut from specific to abstract, proximal to distal. Thus, if some class of direc-
tive representations in a non-human animal or artificial system were to be exclusively
specific and low-level, or exclusively proximal, this would be a notable difference
from the paradigm case, and thus prima facie count against their being intentions.

2.4 Commitment

What distinguishes intentions from other directive states, like desires? One influen-
tial line of thought is that intentions involve a commitment that mere desires do not.
On a standard picture, desires mostly serve as inputs to deliberation (reasoning about
what to do), representing candidate actions the agent is motivated towards. Intentions
are, in the first instance, the outputs of deliberation, and mark the point at which an
agent settles on one possibility from a range of candidates.
   The claim that intentions involve commitment can be separated into three aspects.
First, intentions involve an “executive attitude” (Mele, 1992 p. 162) or are “conduct-
controlling” (Bratman, 1987, p. 16)—all else equal, they will cause one to perform
the intended action, whereas desires are merely potential influencers of action. Sec-

ond, intentions serve to settle among conflicting desires or alternative courses of
action: an agent will thus tend to form a mutually consistent set of intentions, while
the same is not true of desires. Third, intentions have a degree of stability or inertia
(Bratman, 1987). After setting an intention, one normally ceases deliberation on the
relevant issue. Thus intentions act as “terminators of practical reasoning about ends
or goals” (Pacherie & Haggard, 2011, p. 76), and are resistant to reconsideration.8
   The fact that setting an intention implies committing to an action makes it possible
to make further plans on the presumption that one will follow through as intended.
Without commitment, planning would be hostage to significant uncertainty about
one’s own future actions. This brings us to the final characteristic role of intentions,
their role in planning.9

2.5 Planning

A central role for intentions—especially distal intentions—is as part of a broader
system of planning (Bratman, 1987, 2022). Between setting an intention and execut-
ing it, intentions do not remain inert, but “provide crucial inputs for further practical
reasoning and planning” (Bratman, 1987, p. 29) supporting an agent’s ability to coor-
dinate with itself across time. An intention “frames” subsequent deliberation (Brat-
man, 1992) in two ways. First, intentions constrain and prompt means-end reasoning:
if I form an abstract intention like I’ll buy a new suit for the wedding, I now need
to figure out what my budget is, what colour and style I want, when to start looking
in order to have it in time for the wedding. Second, there are horizontal constraints
between intentions: intentions act as a “filter” on the formation of subsequent inten-
tions (Bratman, 1992, p. 1), ruling out courses of action that are inconsistent with
what one already intends (I ought not to intend to head out of town at the same time
I intend to meet with a friend for lunch).10
    In sum, human intentions are characterised by having a directive as opposed to a
descriptive function, exhibiting different degrees of temporal distality and abstrac-
tion, involving commitment to a course of action, and imposing constraints on plan-
ning processes. These features are plausibly interdependent in various ways: For
instance, commitment, in the relevant sense, involves a commitment to do some-
thing, and thus presupposes representations with a directive function. And the more
abstract and distal intentions can be, the more room—and need—there is to ensure
that they are fleshed out and mutually consistent (i.e. planning). Similarly, planning

3 Two case studies: candidates for intentions in LLMs

Before presenting the case studies themselves, it will serve us to briefly review some
of the techniques and methods used in technical AI interpretability research.11 AI
interpretability (or more narrowly, “mechanistic” interpretability) is a research pro-
gramme aimed at illuminating the internal computations of neural networks, includ-
ing transformer-based LLMs. Interpretability researchers seek to explain a model’s
capacities in terms of the components and computational steps that give rise to them.
Central to the programme is the assumption that specific components of activation
vectors—vectors characterising activity across a model’s artificial neurons—repre-
sent task-relevant contents and are causally implicated in the model’s behaviour.
    A key challenge is that individual neurons often respond to multiple unrelated
properties, suggesting that contents are represented in a distributed manner, across
multiple neurons. Sparse autoencoders and related techniques like cross-layer trans-
coders address this by translating activations into sparser encoding schemes, wherein
each neuron’s activity ideally corresponds to a distinct feature leveraged by the
model (Cunningham et al., 2023). Another class of techniques establishes whether
a component plays a hypothesised causal role, by means of intervention: activation
patching replaces an activation with one obtained from a different input (Zhang &
Nanda, 2024), and activation addition (or “steering”) adds a vector to induce a target
behaviour (Turner et al., 2024). These methods can be combined to construct attri-
bution graphs—causal graphs depicting hypothesised computational relationships
between representations (Ameisen et al., 2025).
    In interpreting the results discussed below, which rely on a variety of these inter-
pretability techniques, we should bear in mind that these techniques are relatively
new and their reliability is actively debated (Sharkey et al., 2025; Kantamneni et
al., 2025).12 While causal intervention results and convergence between methods go
some way to validating the findings, it is possible that details of the identified internal
states are somewhat method-dependent, and thus serve as proxies for the latent rep-
resentations that actually explain model behaviour.

3.1 Case study 1: function vectors

The first line of evidence emerges from research into the mechanisms underlying
few-shot in context learning (ICL) in LLMs. Few-shot ICL refers to a language mod-
el’s ability to generate task-appropriate outputs after being given a prompt with a
few examples demonstrating a task. For instance, a model might be given the prompt
“arrive: depart, small: big, common:”. In this example, the implicit relationship
between the words in the two example pairs is that they are antonyms. To continue the
pattern in the examples, the model would need to output an antonym of the cue word
“common”, e.g. “rare”. It turns out that language models of sufficient scale are able
to solve a wide range of tasks based on few-shot examples—e.g. mapping countries
to their currency, mapping people to their occupation, turning present tense to past
tense, and translating words between various languages.
    To identify representations involved in mediating few-shot ICL tasks, Todd and
colleagues (2024) first determined, by means of causal intervention, which attention
heads have the biggest causal effect on performing a given task (such as English-
Spanish translation).13 They then took the most causally relevant heads and, for each
head, calculated the average of its outputs across examples of the task. These aver-
ages were summed together, yielding a vector that the authors refer to as the “function
vector” for that task. The intuition is that, given that the identified attention heads
all play a role in the model’s success at a given task, one can approximately recover
a representation whose role is to induce the model to perform the task by finding
what is invariant in the effective heads’ vector outputs across examples of the task
(and summing them together). And indeed, they were able to broadly validate this
hypothesis, by means of causal interventions with the extracted function vectors:
They showed that injecting a function vector into a model in a new context (a form
of activation patching) triggers execution of the corresponding task robustly across
a wide range of inputs. For instance, they could induce a model to output a Spanish
translation of an English word simply by injecting the function vector extracted from
Spanish-to-English translation examples.14
    In sum, function vectors, the representations discovered by Todd and colleagues,
appear to play a distinctive functional role within the LLMs that they studied, namely,
to represent a certain task and to cause the model to perform that task upon token-
ing of the representation. Thus, it is reasonable to wonder whether they might act as
intentions for the model—i.e. intentions to perform a specific task, such as translate
the English cue-word to Spanish. I will return this question in Sect. 4, where func-

3.2 Case study 2: output and planning representations

Recent work from researchers at Anthropic has explored a set of tools for identifying
and visualising computational mechanisms underlying language model capacities.
Simplifying considerably, Ameisen and colleagues (2025) constructed a modified
version of their target LLM (a “replacement model”), replacing certain parts of the
model with neural network components called cross-layer transcoders. The cross-
layer transcoders are trained to reproduce the input-output profile of the replaced
components, but result in much sparser (and thus easier to interpret) intermediate
activation vectors, which are taken to be proxies for semantically significant dimen-
sions of variation in the original model (so-called “features”).15
    While previous discussions of features primarily focused on representations of
aspects of inputs, Ameisen and colleagues introduce the notion of “output features”,
representations that “promote continuing the response with specific tokens or cat-
egories of closely-related tokens” (Ameisen et al., 2025, § 3.3). For instance, they
identify an output feature to which they attribute the functional role of leading the
model to generate the token “dag”. They cite two lines of evidence for this functional
role: firstly, “it activates strongly on several different tokens, but in each example,
the token is followed by the text ‘dag’.” (Ameisen et al., 2025, § 3.3) And secondly
“the feature increases the probability of the model predicting ‘dag’ more than any
other token (in terms of its direct effect through the residual stream)” (Ameisen et
al., 2025, § 3.3).
    Output features, like the function vectors of Todd and colleagues, already exhibit
some properties that make them candidates for intention-like representations (they
seem to be tightly connected with causing the model to produce particular outputs).
But even more complex functional roles emerge when we turn to Ameisen and col-
leagues’ investigation of computational relations between features. The researchers
used their replacement models to construct “attribution graphs”, hypotheses about
computational relations between features for a given prompt, which they then tested
by means of causal interventions on particular features. For example, in a compan-
ion paper (Lindsey et al., 2025), the researchers investigated how Claude completes
rhyming couplets. E.g. a model was given the following prompt:

4 Assessing the case studies for the five characteristic features

4.1 Directive function?

Do either of the case studies discussed above provide evidence of directive represen-
tations in LLMs? Recall the distinction between role-functions and proper functions.
We can thus assess two things. Firstly, do the representations identified in either case
study play the characteristic causal role of directive representations, i.e. causing the
outcomes that they are hypothesised to represent? Secondly, were they selected for

that causal role? Taking the causal role question first, one source of evidence can
come from interventions on the candidate representation. For instance, exciting or
activating a directive representation of some outcome should make the outcome more
likely to occur, and suppressing it should make the outcome less likely.
   Consider first the case of function vectors. Todd et al. claim that a function vec-
tor, “triggers the execution of a specific procedure by the language model” (p. 1)
and serves as a “generic function-invocation mechanism” (p. 9). Similarly, Hendel
and colleagues argue that “ICL functions by compressing a given training set into a
single task vector, which then guides the transformer to generate appropriate outputs
given queries” (Hendel et al., 2023, p. 9322). The evidence for this is largely based on
interventions. For example, Todd and colleagues inserted function vectors at various
layers for new prompts featuring the cue word, including “zero-shot” contexts (i.e.
the prompt was the cue word and nothing else) and natural language prompts (e.g.
“when I think of ‘x’, I usually…”). Within a certain range of layers, they found that
such interventions led to an increased chance of producing the target output for that
cue word, compared with the outputs of the unaltered model.16
   There is also evidence that output features described by Ameisen and colleagues
play a directive causal role, specifically they “promote continuing the response with
specific tokens or categories of closely-related tokens” (Ameisen et al., 2025, § 3.3).
This was validated with interventions—suppressing an output feature made the cor-
responding output less likely, and activating it made it more likely.17
   Thus, on the face of it, both function vectors and output features have a directive
causal role. However, in both cases, this is only part of the story. Crucially, when
deployed in a chatbot or text generation setting, LLMs have two distinct “phases”
(see Fig. 1). In the prompt processing phase (aka “prefill”), the entire input sequence
is processed but output distributions at each position are discarded. In the token gen-
eration phase (aka “decode”) the output distribution at the final token position is sam-
pled, the sampled token is appended to the input, and the process repeats iteratively.
   The internal processing is identical across both phases. Thus, any given output
feature or function vector active during token generation, will likely also be active
on many occasions during prompt processing. But only during the token generation
phase will these representations cause their represented outcomes to occur. During
prompt processing, if the representations have any determinate function, it is likely
descriptive—anticipating upcoming words in the user-generated sequence rather
than affecting changes via the model’s outputs. This marks a crucial respect in which
LLM representations differ from paradigmatic intentions, which are characterised by
a consistently directive functional role.

4.2 Degrees of distality?

Do we find a spectrum of distal and proximal directive representations in LLMs?
While distality in humans concerns relations in time (between an intention and its
execution), actual time—what computer scientists call “wall-clock time”—is not an
especially useful lens on the operations of an LLM. The time it takes to generate a
sequence of tokens may simply reflect constraints of the underlying hardware, and
further, it is possible to interrupt an LLM mid-generation for an indefinite amount of
time, and then resume generation, picking things up exactly where it left off. Thus,
the distance in actual time between the activation of a representation in an LLM and
the occurrence of some output is a poor measure of the algorithms at play. Neverthe-
less, we can abstract the notion of time somewhat and think of distality and proximal-
ity in terms of token position.
   Consider a token, output by a model at position n in a sequence, midway through
generation. We can ask about the state of the model at position n-1, (i.e. one token
position before n), we can ask about the state of the model at n-2, and so on. Extend-
ing the proximal–distal distinction, proximal intentions would be those occurring
closer in the sequence to their corresponding outputs, and distal intentions those
occurring further away. The most proximal location for an intention to produce a
token at n would be n-1, whereas a distal intention to produce token at n would be a
representation that occurred at an earlier token position (e.g. n-10, n-100).21
   Do the case studies discussed above display gradations of distality? The experi-
ments conducted by Todd and colleagues, do not directly explore degrees of distality,

content beginning at the next token position (thus they are “proximal” in the sense
discussed above). But another class of representations they call “planning features”
appear to be more distal. For example, in the poetry writing case, the researchers
found that the representations of the to-be-written second-line end word (e.g. “rab-
bit”) occurred over the newline token at the end of the first line. In other words, in
at least some instances, representations of future outputs are activated many token
positions earlier in the sequence before those words are output by the model—they
display a degree of distality.
   What remains to be seen is exactly how distal (putative) intentions in language
models can be. The examples discussed above show some initial evidence of direc-
tive representations occurring at most ten-or-so tokens prior to execution. But
noted above, human intentions can be formed with very long time horizons—I can
intend now to do something many decades hence. Could a language model activate
a directive representation that gets executed hundreds or thousands of token posi-
tions later? More empirical work is needed to explore the limits of distality in LLM
representations.

4.3 Degrees of abstraction?

Do the representations identified in the case studies display varying degrees of
abstraction, as is characteristic of intentions? It seems so. Firstly, the function vec-
tors identified by Todd and colleagues appear to be relatively abstract in the sense
that they do not represent specific outputs but relations or functions between words
or concepts. For example, they find function vectors for English to Spanish trans-
lation, such that, when injected into a model that has received any English word
as input, it will induce the model to output the equivalent Spanish word. Another
function vector encodes an abstract notion of next-in-sequence, causing the model
to output the next item in various different kinds of sequences, for instance mapping
“Monday”→“Tuesday”, “December”→“January”, “a”→“b”, and “seven”→“eight”.
Further evidence that the function vectors are abstract comes from the fact that they

induce application of the function across many different prompt formulations. For
instance, in addition to “zero-shot” tests (injecting a function vector when the model
has only received the cue word, “x”), function vectors can induce the desired map-

“While reading a book, I came across the word ‘x’. I looked it up in a dictionary and
it turns out that it means”, and “The word x can be understood as a synonym for”.
    Output features, discussed by Ameisen and colleagues, seem to exhibit a range of
degrees of abstraction. Some are not especially abstract, representing specific tokens
to be output (like the “dag” output feature discussed in Sect. 3.2). Other output fea-
tures, though, are more abstract. One such feature, for example, does not cause the
model to output a specific token, but rather makes the model more likely to output
the name of a game (e.g. “poker”, “billiards”, “scrabble”), another relates to saying a
rhyming word. Another makes the model output a prepositional phrase ending with
a noun. They also found evidence of “multilingual” output features—e.g. a represen-
tation that played a causal role in generating a word meaning “big”, irrespective of
the output language (Ameisen et al., 2025, § 5). Thus, output features often represent
abstract or partially specified plans for action, in much the same way that intentions
do.

4.4 Commitment?

Do representations in LLMs implement the kind of commitment characteristic of
intentions? The poetry writing case may seem to provides some positive evidence:
“rabbit” planning features are tokened several positions before the word is output,
suggesting that the model commits to an end-word in advance. On closer inspection,
however, the story is not so simple. Models do not activate a single planning feature,
instead simultaneously activating representations of alternative end words, including
“habit” and “crab, it”, which the authors conceptualise as “candidate” completions
(Lindsey et al., 2025, § 4). Since these outcomes are not compossible, this falls afoul
of a key dimension of the commitment characteristic of intentions, namely that they
settle among a pool of inconsistent options.
   One might attempt to privilege one of the planning features—for example, the
most strongly active at a given time—as the intention. However, this fails to engage
with the deeper issue. Even less active candidates causally influence the output distri-
bution and thus the output itself. In the case at hand, the model ended the couplet with
“rabbit” only 81% of the time, with “habit” accounting for the remaining 19%. Thus,
the most active directive representation does not appear to “shield” an outcome from
competitors in the way that human intentions shield action execution from distracting
unchosen desires (Cheng et al., 2023; Shah et al., 2002).
   What about the stability or inertia aspect of commitment? Are the candidate rep-
resentations in LLMs resistant to reconsideration in the way that intentions are? Cer-
tainly, LLMs can transport information from earlier token positions to later token
positions via the self-attention mechanism, and this serves as a loose analogue of
maintaining representations across time. However, there is some evidence that, in
certain conditions, transformer based LLMs’ cross-position maintenance of repre-
sentational content breaks down, resulting in un-humanlike behavioural instability.

reading from earlier layers, where “bank” has not yet been properly contextualised
(bank qua financial institution).
   While the authors don’t explicitly distinguish between descriptive and directive
representations in LLMs, it is conceivable that contextualisation errors can occur at
the level of directive representations. That is, at an earlier token position, a model
may form a directive representation to, say, output facts about banks1, but at a later
token position, due to the failure to access the earlier representation, it may form a
directive representation to output facts about banks2. If LLMs display this sort of
instability between token positions, with respect to their directive representations,
then this would be one further respect in which they differ from human intentions.22

dynamics of competition rather than strict logical exclusion.


5 Weighing the evidence

5.1 How well do the candidates fit the profile of intentions?

The foregoing suggests that function vectors (FVs), output features (OFs) and plan-
ning features (PFs) display many of the properties characteristic of human intentions.
And yet there remain some important differences, summarised in Table 1.
    The most notable differences relate to directive functions and commitment. With
respect to directive functions, representations have a directive causal role at most
during the token generation phase. When activated during prompt processing, they
play no such role. Moreover, if we care about the proper function of components,
only LLMs that have been trained on the merit of their textual outputs (as opposed
to merely on their predictions of pre-written text) will have components selected for
output generation. In terms of commitment, planning features and output features do
not settle firmly among inconsistent options, in the way characteristic of intentions:
alternative outcomes are represented alongside each other, jointly and stochastically
influencing model outputs (albeit to different degrees).
    Of course, future empirical research may change our appraisal of the gap between
LLM representations and paradigmatic intentions, with respect to one or other of the
characteristic features—perhaps by uncovering new classes of LLM representations,
or by exploring further instances of the classes of representation discussed here.
And, of course, descendants of current LLMs may close the gap even further—be
it through new training algorithms, augmentations to the basic transformer architec-
ture, or simple increases in scale. Nevertheless, the foregoing gives a sense of our
present state of knowledge on the extent to which the current generation of LLMs
instantiate the characteristic features of intentions.

5.2 Alternative hypotheses

While my primary goal has been to evaluate how well the representations identified
in the case studies fit the functional profile of intentions, the merit of the intention
interpretation can only be fully assessed by comparing it to alternative hypotheses.23
   One rival hypothesis is that the candidate LLM intentions in fact descriptively
represent the nature or structure of a task. It might be argued, for instance, that a
function vector extracted from the English to Spanish translation task does not rep-
resent a directive content like output the Spanish translation of the cue word, but
rather represents that the implicit relationship in each example pair is English->
Spanish or that the user requests a Spanish translation for the cue word. A different
suggestion is that the representations might encode something like procedural knowl-
edge—instructions on how to perform an action or solve a task. (How to identify the

antonym of a word, how to finish a rhyming couplet). Yet another interpretation treats
the representations as tracking whether a goal has been satisfied, for example, the
poem has not yet ended on an appropriate rhyme word.
    While these proposals differ in their details, they all involve ascribing a descrip-
tive, rather than directive role and content to the putative states, and therefore chal-
lenge the suggestion that they are intentions. Accordingly, they all face an additional
explanatory burden: a descriptive representation, by itself, does not typically prompt
a specific action or outcome. Yet in the cases studies, that’s precisely what we see—
activating the putative end poem with “rabbit” planning feature causes the model
to end the poem with "rabbit", and activating the putative translate cue word from
English to Spanish function vector causes the model to do just that. For the other
hypotheses to generate that prediction about the model’s outputs we would need to
posit some additional representational vehicle supplying a directive content. The
“task representation” hypothesis would require positing directives such as continue
the sequence with the implicit relationship exhibited in the example pairs or follow
the user’s request. The procedural knowledge hypothesis would require directives
to perform a certain task now, and thus draw on the merely descriptive procedural
knowledge. And for the goal-satisfaction-tracking hypothesis, this would require a
separate directive representation to specify the goal (qua goal), whose satisfaction is
in need of tracking.
    Three observations about these alternatives. Firstly, they concede that directive
representations are at play somewhere in the mechanism, they just differ on their
precise location and content. Secondly, where the researchers presented evidence
of a broader mechanism (e.g. in the “attribution graphs” calculated by Lindsey and
colleagues) there was no sign of the additional directive representations and compu-
tational steps required by the alternative hypotheses. Finally, the suggestion that the
representations are merely descriptive representations (of one flavour or another) is
at odds with the result that injecting them into new contexts is sufficient to induce
the system to generate the desired output (without needing to also inject a separate
representation of the appropriate directive). Thus, the claim that function vectors,
output features and planning features are intentions (or some species of directive
representation) fares better than these alternative descriptive construal of their role.
    Another, more challenging, alternative holds that the candidate intentions are direc-
tive representations, but of a different sort, such as desires. Certainly, with respect to
commitment (or lack thereof), output features and planning features appear closer
to desires than intentions. However, one obstacle to this interpretation is that, with
respect to planning, these same representations impose intention-like constraints on
the formation of subsequent directive representations. Thus, the representations may
sit awkwardly between the functional profile of intentions and desires.

5.3 Have we found LLM intentions?

So do LLMs really have intentions? What should we conclude from the foregoing
discussion? There may not be a satisfying yes-or-no answer. As has been noted in
discussions of other states and capacities, such as beliefs (Herrmann & Levinstein,
2025), sociality (Strasser & Schwitzgebel 2024) and assertion (Williams & Bayne,

13
Intention-like representations in language models?


2024), psychological concepts often lack sharp boundaries—a fact brought into focus
by emerging AI edge cases. As shown in Sect. 2, there are a range of properties that
characterise human intentions, themselves admitting of gradations and sub-features.
I argued that certain LLM representations exhibit several of these features—some-
times only in a partial or non-standard way—while lacking others. Attempts to say
whether this profile suffices for possession of intentions run into a line-drawing prob-
lem. That’s not to say that a useful line cannot be drawn in some particular theoreti-
cal or social context. For instance, we might decide that in some contexts—e.g. in
generating an explanation of a model’s behaviour for a lay end user—it is useful to
classify planning features (for instance) as intentions. In other contexts, for instance,
when evaluating LLMs as a model of human practical reasoning, we might decide
that the remaining differences are important, and that planning features (or output
features, or function vectors) should not be categorised as intentions. It is beyond the
scope of this paper to attempt to prejudge which contexts call for narrower or wider
boundaries. My goal here was to do the preliminary work of assessing candidates for
intentions in LLMs against the characteristic features of human intentions.


6 Conclusion

Certain representations discovered in recent empirical investigations of LLMs seem
to act somewhat like intentions. I have argued that this is more than just a surface
appearance. A number of features characterise human intentions: directive functions,
degrees of temporal distality and abstraction, commitment to a course of action, and
imposing constraints on planning processes. Many of these features are exhibited by
representations in LLMs. However, there remain important differences between these
representations and human intentions, most notably their failure to play a consistently
directive function, and their failure to firmly settle between inconsistent outcomes.
