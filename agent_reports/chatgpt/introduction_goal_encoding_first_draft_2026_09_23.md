# Introduction draft: establish the encoding, then explain its continuity

September 23, 2026. Revision of the [September 22 draft](introduction_recomputed_goals_draft_2026_09_22.md). The opening establishes the functional question and the evidence for a goal encoding. The discussion of how its content is sustained comes after those findings. No Overleaf files have been changed.

## Proposed introduction

Do LLMs have goals? We often explain a system's behavior in terms of what it is trying to achieve. Such explanations adopt an *intentional stance*, predicting actions in terms of goals and beliefs (Dennett, 1987). We investigate whether these apparent goals correspond to representations within the model: **does an LLM contain an internal encoding of a goal that guides its actions?**

We take a functional view of a goal encoding, asking what it does in the system's computation. In Bratman's planning theory, intentions guide subsequent reasoning and conduct while leaving details of their realization open (Bratman, 1987). Consider the sub-goal “find the length of the longest word” within a larger programming task. This operation can be represented before its implementation begins, realized with a Python expression or a C++ loop, and pursued through many individual steps before completion. Drawing on accounts of intention and goal-directedness, we investigate three corresponding properties: an encoding that **precedes and causally guides action**; **plasticity**, allowing the same goal to be realized in different environments; and **persistence**, sustaining pursuit over the course of execution (Williams, 2026; Lee and McShea, 2020). These properties give us concrete ways to test the relationship between a representation and the behavior it organizes.

We find an encoding with several of these functional properties in the outputs of a sparse set of attention heads. These *goal heads* carry information about the next operation before the model starts implementing it. Their outputs can be used to verbalize that operation or to trigger its implementation in another programming language. The heads also play a causal role in the model's original execution, and their outputs retain goal-specific structure across the tokens that implement the operation. Figure 1 illustrates the connection between these neural representations and the stages of pursuing a sub-goal.

We investigate these findings in a controlled setting with multi-step coding tasks. Each prompt specifies several operations, and a prefilled implementation completes some of them. This lets us distinguish the next *sub-goal* from the broader specification and the work already done. We focus on an intermediate operation, such as finding the longest word's length, that can guide the generation of many tokens. In Williams's (2026) terminology, this representation can be both *proximal*, concerning an action about to begin, and *abstract*, admitting different concrete realizations. Our main experiments use Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct, with readout and transfer replications across model families and sizes.

Motivated by previous work on portable representations of functions and filtering predicates (Todd et al., 2024; Sen Sharma et al., 2026), we localize the goal heads through interventions that transfer an operation between contexts. Building on Patchscopes (Ghandeharioun et al., 2024), we introduce *GoalScope*, which captures these heads' contributions at a source token position and injects them into a separate model run. The probe receives information about the source task only through these activations. With a verbalization prompt, it describes the operation the model is about to perform. With a code prompt in a different programming language, the captured encoding can guide the same operation to be realized through a different sequence of tokens. Both effects are present at positions preceding the operation's implementation. Together, these experiments establish readable goal content before action and its ability to guide an implementation adapted to a new context.

We also test the heads' causal role during ordinary generation. Preserving their access to task information largely preserves the model's ability to implement the next operation when other upper-layer heads lose access to that information. Conversely, restricting a broader set of goal heads sharply impairs implementation. These experiments connect the portable encoding with the model's use of task information in its original context.

Having identified an encoding and tested what it can do, we ask how its content is sustained during execution. Following the goal-head outputs through an implementation, we find that encodings of the same sub-goal remain more similar than encodings of different sub-goals, including comparisons across contexts and programming languages. This temporal continuity concerns the represented operation: the activations themselves change as generation proceeds. A transformer computes new states at each position from the available context, which includes its previous output. The same sub-goal can therefore recur in successive computations as the model takes steps toward completing it. Using the goal heads' value and output projections, *OVReader* additionally traces where this content becomes readable within a forward pass. These observations motivate an account of persistence in which a goal is repeatedly encoded from the evolving context, as illustrated in Figure 1. Persistence under disruption remains a separate question; our models show limited robustness to misleading implementation prefixes, which we examine in the appendix.

Finally, we investigate how the model selects the sub-goal to encode. We propose that it identifies unfinished work by comparing the task specification with its current progress. This offers a possible mechanism for repeatedly recovering the current sub-goal and eventually moving to the next. We test the account through interventions on the goal heads' attention queries and key–value states. Replacing task-related key–value states redirects the operation the model implements; query transfers test how information about completed work changes that selection. The results support a role for both task and progress information, while sensitivity to task order constrains a simple account based on their difference. Our experiments connect an abstract sub-goal with a readable, portable, and causally effective neural encoding, then investigate how the model continues to produce such an encoding as it acts.

## Notes on this revision

- **Order of the argument:** functional question → properties to test → headline finding → controlled setup → readout and plasticity → native causal role → temporal continuity → possible mechanism. The reader encounters the encoding and its effects before being asked to consider how it is reconstructed.
- **Persistence remains an early criterion.** The opening says what sustained pursuit would mean without specifying its implementation. Repeated encoding enters only after the evidence for goal content and causal influence. The phrase “functional persistence through recomputation” no longer serves as the paper's opening thesis.
- **Mechanistic scope:** the later paragraphs distinguish observations from the proposed explanation. Affinity shows goal-specific structure across positions; OVReader shows accessibility across layers; the task/progress comparison is a mechanistic account tested by transfer interventions. This ordering does not turn their conjunction into proof of the full reconstruction mechanism.
- **Terminology:** “precedes and causally guides action” remains the first criterion. It preserves the substantive before-action finding without importing stronger claims about commitment. “Retain goal-specific structure” describes a relationship among changing activations, not storage of an unchanged vector.

Citation keys and the detailed evidence qualifications are in the [previous draft](introduction_recomputed_goals_draft_2026_09_22.md). The philosophical framing and attribution remain as documented in the [research report](goal_framing_research_brainstorm_2026_09_20.md).
