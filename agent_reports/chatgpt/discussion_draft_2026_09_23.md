# Discussion — draft

*Draft against the current Overleaf sources, September 23, 2026. Citation keys and cross-references are retained for eventual insertion. The optional paragraph and editorial notes below are separate from the proposed section.*

We find that LLMs encode an upcoming sub-goal before beginning its implementation, and that this encoding can guide how the operation is realized. The outputs of a small set of attention heads expose this encoding both as a natural-language description and through its effect on generation in another programming language. Restricting the model's access to task information further connects these heads to its native execution. Together, these observations give a concrete basis for studying sub-goals as internal, action-guiding representations.

**An abstraction of the next action.** A sub-goal such as checking for a palindrome admits to many implementations. Transferring its encoding from Python to C++ can trigger the same operation through a different sequence of tokens. This plasticity suggests that the encoding captures an operation at a level of abstraction above its particular realization. Its availability before implementation also gives substance to the idea of intent before action: information about what to do is already present and can influence subsequent generation. The experiments address these functional properties of intention \citep{bratman1987intention,williams2026intention}; they leave open how strongly the model commits to an operation when alternatives compete or circumstances change.

**Functional persistence across generation steps.** The temporal analysis extends this account beyond the initial pivot. Encodings sampled during implementation remain more similar to encodings of the same sub-goal than to those of other sub-goals, including across changes in context and programming language. The continuity is therefore in the represented operation, while the particular activation vector changes with each forward pass. Repeatedly deriving the current sub-goal from the specification and the growing context offers one account of this functional persistence. Establishing that account requires identifying which information supports the encoding at successive positions; similarity alone does not distinguish repeated reconstruction from continued access to earlier states. This motivates interventions that separately disrupt the task specification, the generated implementation, and earlier cached states during execution.

**From a sub-goal encoding to its selection.** DIFF asks how the model selects the next sub-goal by relating the broader goal to progress. The transfer results identify information relevant to that selection: task-span key--value states can redirect the operation, and queries carry information about what the heads look for. However, when the source frontier is also available in the destination, the transplanted query strongly favors that particular operation (\Cref{app:transfer:expb}). Together with OVReader's access to the upcoming operation before most goal heads act, this suggests that selection may already be substantially resolved upstream. The goal heads may help retrieve and communicate a selected operation whose identity was computed earlier. DIFF remains a hypothesis about that earlier computation; identifying its implementation requires separating progress, task order, and operation identity rather than treating query transfer as a selective intervention on progress alone.

**Completion and moving on.** Goal pursuit also requires knowing when to stop pursuing the current sub-goal. Formal accounts permit relinquishing a goal upon believed achievement \citep{cohen1990intention}, and human memory studies find reduced accessibility of intention-related information after completion \citep{marsh1998activation}. The decline in similarity near the implementation boundary is consistent with deactivation or a shift toward another operation. It does not yet identify what causes that transition. A useful next question is whether the model represents the identity of the current sub-goal and its completion status through separable signals, and how these signals interact when selecting the following step.

**An encoding can persist without robust recovery.** Temporal continuity does not guarantee that the model will recover when its implementation is disrupted. Our misleading-prefix experiments show substantial failures, especially for hard prefixes, despite strong performance from aligned starts. This separates the ability to represent an operation from the ability to preserve its influence under conflicting context. The current evaluation concerns local continuations; later correction, explicit deliberation, and tool-assisted revision remain open. Reading the goal encoding during these failures could help distinguish losing track of the intended operation from retaining it but failing to realize it. Interventions that restore the encoding would then test whether that distinction explains recovery.

**Scope of the readout.** The coding setup supplies explicit task descriptions and a known implementation boundary, making the selected sub-goal unusually well specified. Readouts in ICL and chat settings extend the method to operations inferred from context, although success varies and descriptions of composed tasks are often incomplete. GoalScope also uses a separate generation process to interpret the captured state, so the description depends on the probe as well as the source encoding. Our oracle scores measure semantic agreement and can credit partial implementations; they do not establish full program correctness. Similarly, the native access interventions identify an important route for task information while leaving other computation intact. The distinction between a small set supporting readout and a broader set whose restriction impairs execution is relevant to how widely the encoding is distributed.

These findings make it possible to investigate goal pursuit at the level of individual generation steps. The next challenge is to connect the emergence of a sub-goal encoding, its influence during implementation, and its replacement at completion within one causal account. Such an account would explain both how the model follows a task and where that process fails.

## Optional paragraph if the boundary experiments return

*This would replace the last two sentences of “Completion and moving on.” The current appendix explicitly marks the boundary section for removal and does not include it in the compiled paper.*

Our exploratory boundary interventions provide a starting point (\Cref{app:boundary}). A signal captured at completed steps can promote advancing to a new variable across changes in variable names, with a stronger effect through the selected boundary heads than through the goal heads. This suggests a possible distinction between the content of an operation and the decision to advance. Testing whether the intervention also changes the goal readout and produces a correct subsequent implementation would clarify whether it controls a semantic transition between sub-goals.

## Notes for review

- The draft treats recomputation as a mechanistic hypothesis motivated by continuity, without relying on the layer-budget necessity argument.
- The DIFF paragraph incorporates Experiment B and OVReader, while retaining the broader goal-versus-progress account as a candidate upstream mechanism.
- “Completion and moving on” distinguishes the observed decline near the boundary from the human finding of reduced accessibility after completion. The current LM analysis does not establish active suppression or release to prevent interference.
- I did not include the existing comment about code comments as an empirical explanation: the source prompt requests code without comments, and the current experiments do not isolate whether the heads also produce comments. Comparing their role in comments and executable code could be a separate future direction.
